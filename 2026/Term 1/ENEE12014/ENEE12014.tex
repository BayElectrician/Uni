	\subsection{Essential Points:}
	Node: A point in a circuit which two or more elements join.\\
	Essential Node: Where three or more elements join.\\
	Path: A trace of adjoining basic elements with no repition.\\
	Branch: A path connecting two nodes.\\

	Mesh: A loop that does not enclose another loop.
	\subsection{Nodal Analysis:}
	Nodal analysis is known as the node-voltage method. This is a method calculating node voltages in a planar circuit. It utilizes the fundamentals of KCL and Ohm's Law.
	\subsubsection{Procedure}
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item Identify each essential node.
		\item Pick and label a reference node (usually ground), then label the node voltages at the remaining essential nodes.
		\item Write KCL equations for each non-reference essential node.
		\item Solve equations.
		\item Find voltages, power and current at each node.
	\end{enumerate}

	\section{Mesh Analysis}
	\subsection{Branch Current Analysis:}
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item Assign a distinct current of arbitrary direction to each direction.
		\item Polarities of circuit elements are determined by assumed current direction.
		\item Apply KVL around each loop.
		\item Apply KCL at each essential node.
		\item Solve for current values.
	\end{enumerate}
	\subsection{Mesh Analysis:}
	Extension of the above method. It defines currents in such a way that there is no need to apply KCL.
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Define currents for each mush of the networks.
		\item Branch current is now expressed in terms of unique mesh currents.
	\end{itemize}
	\subsubsection{Procedure}
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item Assign distinct current to each mesh. The easiest way is have them all going in the same direction.
		\item Indictate polarities through the loop with respect to current direction.
		\item Apply KVL on each mesh.
		\item Solve for currents.
	\end{enumerate}

	\section{RC Circuits}
	\subsection{Capacitors:}
	Constructed using a dialectric material middle, sandwhiched between two conducting plates. As it charges an electric field is generated. As such its true characteristics are shown when a change in voltage or current take places. To a DC source there is zero change in current because capacitors act an O/C.
	$$i=C\frac{dv}{dt}$$
	Voltage cannot change instantaneously as it results in infinite induced current. It takes time for voltage to reach desired voltage and vice versa to reach 0V.
	\begin{align*}
		i=&C\frac{dv}{dt}\\
		idt=&Cdv\\
		dv=&\frac{1}{C}idt\\
		\int^{v(t)}_{v(t_{0})}1dx=&\frac{1}{C}\int^{t}_{t_{0}}idx\\
		v(t)-v(t_{0})=&\frac{1}{C}\int^{t}_{t_{0}}idx\\
		v(t)=&\frac{1}{C}\int^{t}_{t_{0}}idx+v(t_{0})
	\end{align*}
	\begin{align*}
		p=&vi\\
		=&vC\frac{dv}{dt}\\
		=&i[\frac{1}{C}\int^{t}_{t_{0}}idx+v(t_{0})]\\
		W=&\frac{Cv^{2}}{2}
	\end{align*}
	Power is in Watts and Energy (W) is in Joules.
	\subsection{Equivalent Capacitence:}
	\subsubsection{Series}
	$$C_{eq}=\frac{1}{\sum^{k}_{n=1}\frac{1}{C_{n}}}$$
	$$v(t_{0})=v_{1}(t_{0})+v_{2}(t_{0})+...+v_{n}(t_{0})$$
	\subsubsection{Parallel}
	$$C_{eq}=\sum^{k}_{n=1}C_{n}$$
	\subsection{Inductors:}
	Inductors can be thought of as the dual to the capacitor. Similar to the capacitor, inductors only exhibit true behaviour on current and voltage changes. Inductors are created by wrapping wire material around a ferromagnetic core. As such they are designed to generate magnetic field linking the unit. Inductors need a change in current to induce voltage. A constant current (DC) would induce OV.
	\begin{align*}
		v=&L\frac{di}{dt}\\
		vdt=&Ldi\\
		L\int^{i(t)}_{i(t_{0})}1dx=&\int^{t}_{t_{0}}vdy\\
		i(t)-i(t_{0})=&\frac{1}{L}\int^{t}_{t_{0}}vdy\\
		i(t)=&\frac{1}{L}\int^{t}_{t_{0}}vdy+i(t_{0})
	\end{align*}
	\begin{align*}
		p=&vi\\
		=&iL\frac{di}{dt}\\
		=&v[\frac{1}{L}\int^{t}_{t_{0}}vdy+i(t_{0})]\\
		w=&\frac{Li^{2}}{2}
	\end{align*}

	\section{RLC Circuits - Parallel}
	\subsection{Second Order Differential Equations:}
	RLC circuits consist of $2^{\text{nd}}$ ODEs in homogeneous constant coefficient equations.
	$$\frac{d^{2}y}{dx^{2}}+A\frac{dy}{dx}+By=0$$
	$$s^{2}+As+B=0$$
	$$s=\frac{-A\pm\sqrt{A^{2}-4B}}{2}$$
	$$\therefore y=k_{1}e^{s_{1}t}+k_{2}e^{s_{1}t}$$
	$k_{1}$ \& $k_{2}$ are found in the initial conditions within the circuit.

	\subsection{Under-damped:}
	$$(\frac{1}{2RC})^{2}-\frac{1}{LC}<0$$
	\begin{align*}
		s&=-\alpha+\sqrt{\alpha^{2}-\omega_{0}^{2}}\\
		     &=-\alpha+j\sqrt{\omega_{0}^{2}-\alpha^{2}}\\\\

		s_{2}&=-\alpha-j\omega_{d}
	\end{align*}
	This is where $\omega_{d}$ is the damped radian frequency.
	$$v(t)=k_{1}e^{-\alpha t}\cos{(\omega_{d}t)}+k_{2}e^{-\alpha t}\sin{(\omega_{d}t)}$$
	$$V_{0}=k_{1}$$
	$$C(-k_{1}\alpha+k_{2}\omega_{d})=-(I_{0}+\frac{V_{0}}{R})$$
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Oscillatory with period damped radian frequency.
		\item Amplitude decreases at the rate $\alpha$, this is called the damping factor.
		\item Oscillates due to two stored energies.
		\item Overdamped - Takes times to reach final value, but transients do not exceeed intitial value.
		\item Underdamped - Reaches final value, oscilates around it before reaching steady state. Transient spikes can be few orders of magnitude larger then initial values.
	\end{itemize}

	\subsection{Impedance:}
	Basically resistance within AC circuits.
	$$v=Zi$$
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Resistor - $Z_{R}=R=R\angle{0^{\circ}}$
		\item Inductor - $Z_{L}=j\omega L=\omega L\angle{90^{\circ}}=2\pi fL\angle{90^{\circ}}$
		\item Capacitor - $Z_{C}=\frac{-j}{\omega C}=\frac{1}{\omega C}\angle{-90^{\circ}}=\frac{1}{2\pi fC}\angle{-90^{\circ}}$
	\end{itemize}
	Although $Z$ can be a complex number, it is NOT a phasor. Resistance is the real part, whereas reactance is the imaginary part. Admittance is the inverse of impedance:
	$$Y=\frac{1}{Z}$$
	\subsection{Equivalent Impedance:}
	\subsubsection{Series}
	$$Z_{eq}=\sum^{k}_{n=1}Z_{n}$$
	\subsubsection{Parallel}
	$$Z_{eq}=\frac{1}{\sum^{k}_{n=1}\frac{1}{Z_{n}}}$$

		v=&V_{m}\cos{(\omega t+\theta_{v})}\\
		i=&I_{m}\cos{(\omega t+\theta_{i})}\\\\
		v=&V_{m}\cos{(\omega t+\theta_{v}-\theta_{i})}\\
		i=&I_{m}\cos{(\omega t)}\\\\
		\therefore p=&V_{m}\cos{(\omega t+\theta_{v}-\theta_{i})}\times I_{m}\cos{(\omega t)}\\
		=&\frac{V_{m}I_{m}}{2}\cos{(\theta_{v}-\theta_{i})}+\frac{V_{m}I_{m}}{2}\cos{(\theta_{v}-\theta_{i})}\cos{(2\omega t)}\\
		 &-\frac{V_{m}I_{m}}{2}\sin{(\theta_{v}-\theta_{i})}\sin{(2\omega t)}
	\end{align*}
	It is important to note that the frequency of power is double that of voltage and current. Also at certain points power is a negative value.
	\subsection{Average Power:}
	\begin{align*}
		p=&\frac{1}{T}\int^{t_{0}+T}_{t_{0}}vi\;dt\\
		=&\frac{V_{m}I_{m}}{2}\cos{(\theta_{v}-\theta_{i})}
	\end{align*}
	This is also called real power and is measured in Watts (W).
	\subsection{Reactive Power:}
	\begin{align*}
		Q=&\frac{V_{m}I_{m}}{2}\sin{(\theta_{v}-\theta_{i})}\\
		=&\lvert S \rvert\sqrt{1-pf^{2}}
	\end{align*}
	Called reactive power and measured in Volt-Amperes-Reactive (VAR).
	\subsection{Pure Values:}
	\subsubsection{Resistor}
	For resistor voltage and current are in the same phase.
	$$\therefore\;\theta_{v}=\theta_{i}$$
	$$P=\frac{V_{m}I_{m}}{2},\;\;Q=0$$
	$$p=P(1+\cos{(2\omega t)}$$
	Power is always a positive value, this means it always dissapates.
	\subsubsection{Inductor}
	Current lags voltage by $90^{\circ}$
	$$\therefore\;\theta_{v}-\theta_{i}=90^{\circ}$$
	$$P=0\;\;Q=\frac{V_{m}I_{m}}{2}$$
	$$p=-Q\sin{(2\omega t)}$$
	The average power is 0. Thus, in the positive cycle, energy is stored in the inductor, with the negative cycle, energy is released from the indcutor.
	\subsubsection{Capacitor}
	Current leads voltage by $90^{\circ}$
	$$\therefore\;\theta_{v}-\theta_{i}=-90^{\circ}$$
	$$P=0\;\;Q=-\frac{V_{m}I_{m}}{2}$$
	$$p=Q\sin{(2\omega t)}$$
	Same cycle types as the inductor by reversed. Basically, when the capacitor releases the inductor absorbs, and the capacitor absorbs the inductor releases.
	\subsection{Power Factor:}
	$$\cos{(\theta_{v}-\theta_{i})}$$
	Known as the power fator, the angle describes the angle\\
	Lagging pf: $\theta_{v}-\theta_{i}>0$\\
	Leading pf: $\theta_{v}-\theta_{i}<0$
	\subsection{RMS Value:}
	This is mostly known as the effective value.
	\begin{align*}
		P=&V_{RMS}I_{RMS}\cos{(\theta_{v}-\theta_{i})}\\
		=&V_{eff}I_{eff}\cos{(\theta_{v}-\theta_{i})}\\\\
		Q=&V_{eff}I_{eff}\sin{(\theta_{v}-\theta_{i})}
	\end{align*}
	\subsection{Complex Power:}
	$$S=P+jQ$$
	Complext power as units Volt-Amperes (VA) and is dimensionally akin to the average power. The $\lvert s \rvert$ value is the known as apparent power. It has the same units as complext power.

	\subsection{PF Correction:}
	Power dissapated in transmission lines depends on current. Hence, current through the lines should be the smallest values possible. $S$ should be as close to 0 as possible.
	$$P=S\cos{(\theta)}$$
	$$pF=\cos{(\theta)}=\frac{P}{S}$$
	$$\theta=\arccos{(\frac{P}{S})}$$
