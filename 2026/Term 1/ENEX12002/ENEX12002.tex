	\subsection{Analog \& Digital Circuits:}
	Analog circuits are used in situations implementing linear functions, wave shaping and transformation between current and voltage. Digital circuit however ase used to process data rapidly and in calculations. Most systems utilize a mix of both systems (e.g AV systems).
	\subsection{Sampling:}
	When converting between signal types sampling must take place. Sampling is the approximation of analog's original waveform into time slices. The disadvantage of sampling is that is loses some precision, however the advantages of a digital system out weight this. The physical process of sampling is called quantizing. This produces numbers that can be processed by digital systems.

	\subsection{Vectors:}
	A vector is a line that contains both direction and magnitude. An AC circuits sinusodial waveform can be generated by plotting the project of a rotating vector in circular motion.
	\subsection{Frequency and Period:}
	The frequency of a periodic wave is the number of cycles that occur every second. The SI unit for this is Hz. The period is how long it takes a waveform to complete one cycle.
	$$f=\frac{1}{T}$$
	$$T=\frac{1}{f}$$

	\subsection{Waveform Values:}
	The amplitude is the maximum distance of the values from the line of equilibrium. The average value of a sine wave is the found in $\lvert v(t) \rvert$, then averaging the curve.
	$$V_{ave}=\frac{2V_{p}}{\pi}\approx 0.637V_{p}$$
	\subsection{RMS Values:}
	In order to compare AC and DC currents and voltages, AC is converted into its DC equivalent DC heating value. RMS current and voltage are their peak values.
	\begin{align*}
		V_{RMS}=&\frac{V_{p}}{\sqrt{2}}\\
		\approx & 0.707V_{p}\\\\
		I_{RMS}=&\frac{I_{p}}{\sqrt{2}}\\
		\approx & 0.707I_{p}
	\end{align*}

	\subsection{Transducers:}
	These are a device that converts a physical quantity (e.g pressure, position or temp) from one form to another. Their outupts are quite small and require amplification. Passice transducers require external power to functions, this is called excitation. Active transducers are self-generating that convert a portion being measured into electrical signal.
	\subsection{Amplification:}
	Amps are designde to amplify either voltage or power. Linear amplifiers produce a magnified replica of the inputted signal, producing a useful output.
	$$A_{v}=\frac{V_{out}}{V_{in}}$$
	\subsection{Transfer Curves:}
	All amplifiers, when ideal, produce a linear transfer curves. Beyond this limit they no longer act idealy, this is called saturation.
	\subsection{Power Gain:}
	$$A_{p}=\frac{P_{out}}{P_{in}}=\frac{V_{out}I_{out}}{V_{in}I_{in}}$$
	\subsection{dB Power Ratios:}
	The decibal of a power ratio is:
	\begin{align*}
		A_{p}=&10\log_{10}{(\frac{P_{1}}{P_{2}})}\\
		=&10\log_{10}{(A_{p})}
	\end{align*}
	\subsection{dB Voltage Ratio:}
	\begin{align*}
		A_{v}=&20\log_{10}{(\frac{V_{1}}{V_{2}})}\\
		=&20\log_{10}{(A_{v})}
	\end{align*}

	\subsection{Input Offset Voltage:}
	The ideal Op-Amp produces $0V_{out}$ for $0V_{in}$. However, practically a small DC voltage, $V_{out(error)}$ is created. On the datasheet, the input offset voltage ($V_{os}$), is the voltage required to force an output. Typically $V_{os}<2mV$. A key note is that $V_{os}$ drifts with temperature.

	\section{Number Systems}
	\subsection{Decimal Number System:}
	Used everywhere within the modern world. It is also known as the arabic number system or Base 10. For example:
	$$128.55=1\times10^{2}+2\times10^{1}+8\times10^{0}+5\times10^{-1}+6\times10^{-2}$$
	\subsection{Binary Number System:}
	Represents numbers in a similar way to Base10 but only with the numbers of 1 and 0. For example:
	$$1001.01=1\times2^{3}+0\times2^{2}+0\times2^{1}+1\times2^{0}+0\times2^{-1}+1\times2^{-2}$$
	\subsection{Binary to Decimal Conversion:}
	Use expansion method (shown above), which will just give then Base10 value. This method is also known as a weighted sum.
	\subsection{Decimal to Binary Conversion:}
	Just succesively divide it by the highest two and keep remainder.
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item Divide the Base10 number into its whole and fraction parts
		\item For the whole successively divide by 2 until 0r0 or 0r1 is found
		\item Fractions multiply by 2 until it is 1r0
	\end{enumerate}

	\subsection{Arithmetic Operations:}
	\subsubsection{Addition and Subtraction}
	The same principles used within the Base10 system are in the Base2.
	\subsubsection{Multiplication}
	Start multiplying with respect to the start position.
	\subsubsection{Division}
	In Base2, division is just a bunch of subtractions until a set remainder remains.

	\subsection{Signed Numbers:}
	It is useful to have a signed number to know the positive and negative numbers. Since Base2 has no default way of designating positive and negative values, an extra bit is used to denote this. Since there is no standard implementation of this extra bit, the exact position has to be agreed on before hand.

	\subsection{Complements:}
	\subsubsection{1's Complement}
	Flip the bits. E.g 1001 turns to 0110.
	\subsubsection{2's Complement}
	The 1's complement and +1. 1001 turns to 0110 + 1 = 0111. Always equal to $2^{n}$ the number of bits.\\
	Positive Range: $000\ldots00\rightarrow011\ldots11$\\
	Negative Range: $111\ldots11\rightarrow100\ldots00$
	\subsubsection{Subtraction}
	Addition, but the subtracted value has a changed signed bit.
	\subsubsection{Multiplication and Division}
	Multiply and divide normally in true form and find out the sign of the product.

	\subsection{Hexadecimal:}
	Uses a Base16 system (0-9, and A-F). Converting B-Hex, group the bits into 4's and calculate the hex equivalent. Dec-Hex is just divide and multiply by 16.
	\subsection{Octal:}
	Base8 system that uses 0-7.
	\subsection{Binary Coded Decimal:}
	This is an interface between binary and decimal values. Each 10's column is a 4-bit binary value. Values 10-15 are invalid, where once 10 is reached, it goes to the next 4-bit grouping.

	\end{tabular}
	\end{center}

Transitions from one code to another changing only 1-bit at a time. This is because changing multiple bit an introduce glitches if they are not synchronised.

	\subsection{12 Rules of Boolean Algebra:}
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item $A+0=A$
		\item $A+1=1$
		\item $A\cdot0=0$
		\item $A\cdot1=A$
		\item $A+A=A$
		\item $A+\overline{A}=1$
		\item $A\cdot A=A$
		\item $A\cdot\overline{A}=0$
		\item $\overline{\overline{A}}=A$
		\item $A+AB=A$
		\item $A+\overline{A}B=A+B$
		\item $(A+B)(A+C)=A+BC$
	\end{enumerate}

	\subsection{Logic Simplification:}
	When the logic is simple it is both more efficient and convient to implement. Methods to do this are:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Boolean Algebra, 12 Laws and DeMorgan's Theorem
		\item Karnaugh Map
		\item Quine-McCluskey
		\item Espresso Algorithm
	\end{itemize}
	Repeat until it just loops through same types. This means the expression is in its simpliest form. For example:
	\begin{align*}
		E&=(AB'(C+BD)+A'B')C\\
		&=(AB'C+AB'BD+A'B')C\\
		&=(AB'C+A'B')C\\
		&=AB'CC+A'B'C\\
		&=AB'C+A'B'C\\
		&=(A+A')B'C\\
		&=B'C
	\end{align*}

	\subsection{Standard Forms:}
	\subsubsection{Sum of Product}
	Each product term of sum include all variables, using $A+A'=1$.
	\begin{align*}
		&AB+AB'C\\
		=&AB(C+C')+AB'C\\
		=&ABC+ABC'+AB'C
	\end{align*}
	\subsubsection{Product of Sum}
	Each sum term of the product includes all variables, using $A=(A+B)(A+B')$.
	\begin{align*}
		&(A+B)(A+B'+C)\\
		=&(A+B+C)(A+B+C')(A+B'+C)
	\end{align*}\\[1\baselineskip]
	The SOP and POS forms are directly linked to the truth tables outputs.

	A systematic method to minimise SOP and POS expressions. Similar to a truth table:

		\item Adjacent Cells: Only change by 1-bit

	\begin{enumerate}

		\item AND-OR-NOT (POS)
		\item XOR
		\item XNOR
	\end{enumerate}
