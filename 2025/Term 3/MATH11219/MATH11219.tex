	\section{Differentiation}
	Differentiation (Derivatives) is just a mathematical expression of a functions rate-of-change. Think of the gradient of a function, area/estimation of a function, kinematics \& optimisation of area or numbers.
	\subsection{Average Rate-of-Change (ARoC):}
	The ARoC of the function is just the estimation of a curve based on a linear lines gradient. I.E. the average gradient between two points.
	$$\text{ARoC}=\frac{y_{2}-y_{1}}{x_{2}-x_{1}}$$
	\subsection{Instaneous Rate-of-Change (IRoC):}
	The ARoC of a function is good enough to understand the general direction in which a graph is continuing. Its accuracy can be improved by limiting the amount of change between $x_{1}$ \& $x_{2}$. However a problem occurs when $\Delta x\rightarrow0$. As cannot divide by 0. To find a functions IRoC, use the derivatives by first principles.
	\begin{align*}
		f(x)&=3x^{2}+2\\
		f'(x)&=\lim_{\delta x \to 0}\frac{f(x+\delta x)-f(x)}{\delta x}\\
		     &=\lim_{\delta x \to 0}\frac{[3(x+\delta x)^{2}+2]-[3x^{2}+2]}{\delta x}\\
		     &=\lim_{\delta x \to 0}\frac{[3x^{2}+6x\delta x+3\delta x^{2}+2]-[3x^{2}+2]}{\delta x}\\
		     &=\lim_{\delta x \to 0}\frac{6x\delta x+3\delta x^{2}}{\delta x}\\
		     &=\lim_{\delta x \to 0}6x+3\delta x\\
		     &=6x
	\end{align*}
	\subsection{Limits in Functions:}
	Specifically in Piece-wise functions there can be an issue with derivatives and their interpretations. This is mostly around when the Left and Right Hand Limits not meeting on the same point, making the functions discontinuous.

	\section{Techniques of Differentiation}
	\subsection{Product Rule:}
	The product rule is used to differentiate two functions multiplied together.
	\begin{align*}
		y=&f(x)g(x) \\
		\frac{dy}{dx}=&f'(x)g(x)+f(x)g'(x)\\\\
		y=&uv\\
		\frac{dy}{dx}=&u'v+v'u
	\end{align*}
	\subsection{Quotient Rule:}
	This rule is used when two functions divide each other. It is important to know that the order of functions matter with this rule. As this subtracts rather then addition.
	\begin{align*}
		y&=\frac{u}{v}\\
		\frac{dy}{dx}&=\frac{u'v-v'u}{v^{2}}
	\end{align*}
	\subsection{Chain Rule:}
	By using differentiation by substitution (A.K.A) chain rule, function within other functions can be derived.
	\begin{align*}
		y=&f(g(x))\\
		\frac{dy}{dx}=&g'(x)f'(g(x))
	\end{align*}

	\subsection{Implicit Differentiation}
	This is used when the equation is not simply $y=f(x)$, e.g.:
	\begin{align*}
		x^{2}-y^{3}+\sin{(x)}-\cos{(y)}&=1 \\\\
		\frac{d}{dx}(x^{2})&=2x \\
		\frac{d}{dx}(-y^{3})&=-3y^{2}\frac{dy}{dx}\\
		\frac{d}{dx}(\sin{(x)})&=\cos{(x)}\\
		\frac{d}{dx}(-\cos{(y)})&=\sin{(y)}\frac{dy}{dx}\\
		\frac{d}{dx}(1)&=0\\\\
		\frac{d}{dx}(x^{2}-y^{3}+\sin{(x)}-\cos{(y)})&=\frac{d}{dx}1\\
		2x-3y^{2}\frac{dy}{dx}+\cos{(x)}+\sin{(y)}\frac{dy}{dx}&=0\\
		\frac{dy}{dx}&=\frac{-2x-\cos{(x)}}{\sin{(y)}-3y^{2}}\\
			     &=\frac{2x+\cos{(x)}}{3y^{2}-\sin{(y)}}
	\end{align*}

	\subsection{Parametric Differentiation}
	Basically just multiplying the derivatives together.
	$$\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{\frac{dy}{dt}}{\frac{dx}{dt}}$$

	\pagebreak

	\subsection{Logarithmic Differentiation}
	Just follow the basic steps and the derivative will be found. Mostly useful for a short version of the product rule.
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item Find the log of each side of the equation, \& use log laws to expand the equation
		\item Differentiate each side with respect too the independent variable
		\item Rearrange and substitute
		\item Simplify
	\end{enumerate}
	For Example,
	\begin{align*}
		y=&t^{2}(1+t)^{3}\\\\
		\ln{(y)}=&\ln{(t^{2}[1+t]^{3})}\\
			=&\ln{(t^{2})}+\ln{([1+t]^{3})}\\
			=&2\ln{(t)}+3\ln{(1+t)}\\\\
		\frac{d}{dt}\ln{(y)}=&\frac{d}{dt}[2\ln{(t)}+3\ln{(1+t)}]\\
		\frac{1}{y}\frac{dy}{dt}=&\frac{2}{t}+\frac{3}{1+t}\\
		\frac{dy}{dt}=&y(\frac{2}{t}+\frac{3}{1+t})
	\end{align*}
	
	\subsection{Higher Derivatives}
	Found by differentiating the function multiple times.\\
	These are useful as they can describe the nature of a function, such as\\
	If $\frac{dy}{dx}>0$, the gradient can be said to be increasing, or $\frac{dy}{dx}<0$, the gradient is decreasing and $\frac{dy}{dx}=0$ the gradient is stable.\\
	Or $\frac{d^{2}y}{dx^{2}}>0$, concavity is up and the point (x,y) is described as a minimum. $\frac{d^{2}y}{dx^{2}}=0$, described as a Point of Inflection. Basically just the point where the concavity of a function changes. $\frac{d^{2}y}{dx^{2}}<0$, concave down and point (x,y) is a maximum.

	\section{Sequences \& Series:}
	\subsection{Sequences}
	A sequence is a set of numbers in a particular order with most cases following a pattern.\\
	E.g. $x[k]=(-1)^{k+1}\cdot\frac{1}{k},\;\forall k\geq 1$ becomes,\par
	$1$, $\frac{-1}{2}$, $\frac{1}{3}$, \ldots

	\subsection{Series}
	Series are the sum of multiple sequences up until  a certain point, eg.
	$$\sum^{5}_{n=1}(2n-1)=1+3+5+7+9$$

	\subsection{Convergence}
	If an infinite series is said to converge upon a value if and only if it tends towards a singular value. For example $\lim_{k\to\infty}\;\frac{1}{k}$ converges towards 0. A sequence can converge on a non-zero quantity. For a point that might be considered to converge on $\infty$ is instead called to diverge. E.g. $\lim_{k\to\infty}\sqrt{k}=\infty$.
	$$x[k]=\frac{3k^{2}+2k-7}{9k^{2}-7k},\;k\to\infty$$
	\begin{align*}
		&\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{3k^{2}+2k-7}{9k^{2}-7k}\\
		=&\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{(3k^{2}+2k-7)\times\frac{1}{k^{2}}}{(9k^{2}-7k)\times\frac{1}{k^{2}}}\\
		=&\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{3+\frac{2}{k}-\frac{7}{k^{2}}}{9-\frac{7}{k}}\\
		=&\frac{3}{9}\;\text{, because}\;\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{a}{k}=0\\
		=&\frac{1}{3}
	\end{align*}

	\subsection{Arithmetic Sequences}
	This is a type of sequence in which each values difference is the same amount.
	$$a_{n}=a_{1}+(n-1)d$$
	These vaues can also be summed to a total series. The proof of this is beautiful thing to see, look it up online.
	$$S_{n}=\frac{n}{2}(2a_{1}+[n-1]d)$$

	\subsection{Geometric Sequences}
	This a sequence of numbers related to a common ratio of $r$.
	$$a_{n}=a_{1}\cdot r^{n-1}$$
	$$S_{n}=\frac{a_{1}(1-r)}{1-r},\;r\neq 1$$
	If $-1\leq r\leq 1$,
	$$S_{n}=\frac{a_{1}}{1-r}$$
	This is also the only one that can find $S_{\infty}$. Outside of $-1\leq r\leq 1$ $r^{n}$ fails to converge on a finite limit as $n\to\infty$, thus the series and sequence diverges.

	\subsection{Approximating Points on a Function}
	Think amount the point (Q) on a function. When an approximation of the function is close to Q it is considered to be fairly accurate. However as the distance between the approximation and Q grows.
	$$f(x)=f(a)+f'(a)(x-a)$$

	\subsection{Taylor Series}
	Taylor Series becomes more accurate the higher order (highest power in the set) is.\\
	Zero: $p_{0}(x)=f(a)$\\
	First: $p_{1}(x)=f(a)+f'(a)(x-a)$\\
	Second: $p_{2}(x)=f(a)+f'(a)(x-a)+\frac{f''(a)}{2!}(x-a)^{2}$\\
	Continues with higher orders

	\subsection{Remainder Term}
	This is the error in the $n^{th}$ term of a Taylor Polynomial.
	$$f(x)=p_{n}(x)+R_{n}(x)$$
	$$R_{n}(x)=\frac{y^{n+1}(c)(x-a)}{(n+1)!},\;\text{where}\;a<c<x$$

	\subsection{Maclaurin Series}
	Think of this as a Taylor polynomial, except that it rotates around the point $x=0$.
	$$f(x)=f(0)+f'(0)x+\frac{f''(0)}{2!}+\frac{f^{(3)}(0)}{3!}x^{3}+\ldots$$

	\section{Techniques of Integration}
	\subsection{Integration by Parts:}
	When thinking about the product rule ($\frac{d}{dx}(uv)=u'v+v'u$), integration by parts is a way of doing a reverse product rule.
	$$\int u'v=uv-\int uv'$$
	The key to solving the above equations is making smart choices about which parts of the functions are $u$ and $v$. The following guide can be utilized when making that descision:\\
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item Logs
		\item Inverse Trigonometric
		\item Algebraic (Polynomials)
		\item Normal Trigonometric
		\item Exponential
	\end{enumerate}
	For Example:
	\begin{align*}
		\int(5x+1)&\cos{(2x)}dx\\
		\text{let}\;u=5x&+1\;\;\;v'=cos(2x)\\
		u'=&5\;\;\;\;\;v=\frac{1}{2}\sin{(2x)}\\
		\therefore\int uv'=&uv-\int u'v\\
		=&\frac{1}{2}(5x+1)\sin{(2x)}-\int\frac{5}{2}\sin{(2x)}dx\\
		=&\frac{1}{2}(5x+1)\sin{(2x)}+\frac{5}{4}\cos{(2x)}+c
	\end{align*}
	\subsection{Integration via Substitution:}
	This allows a chain rule derivative to be integrated. It does this by splitting the terms down into usable terms. However, it is important to note that the seeminly obvious solution is not always the correct one. E.g.
	\pagebreak
	$$\int x\sqrt{1-x^{2}}dx$$
	\begin{align*}
		\text{let}\;u=&1-x^{2}\\
		\frac{du}{dx}=&-2x\\
		dx=&-\frac{du}{2x}
	\end{align*}
	\begin{align*}
		\int x\sqrt{1-x^{2}}dx=&\int x\sqrt{u}(-\frac{du}{2x})\\
		=&-\frac{1}{2}\int\sqrt{u}du\\
		=&-\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{2}{3}u^{\frac{3}{2}}+c\\
		=&-\frac{1}{3}(1-x^{2})^{\frac{3}{2}}+c
	\end{align*}
	\subsection{Integrals in Forms:}
	When $\int\frac{f'(x)}{f(x)}=\ln{\|f(x)\|}+c$. This can just be a simple way of immediately recognizing an integration via substitution.
	\subsection{Integration via Partial Fractions:}
	Turn a integral not in form (i.e. not substitution) into standard natural log form. This can then be integrated as normal.

	\section{Further Integration}
	\subsection{Average Value of a Function:}
	Often wanting to know the average value within a function is useful. The independent variable within mostly a period of time over a specified interval. The main example of this is time. I.E. finding the average velocity of car going certain speeeds.\\
	$$\overline{x}=\frac{1}{b-a}\int^{b}_{a}f(x)dx$$
	\\
	E.g. Find the Average value of $f(t)=t^{2}$, $t\in[1,3]$
	\begin{align*}
		AV=&\frac{1}{3-1}\int^{3}_{1}t^{2}dt\\
		=&\frac{1}{2}[\frac{1}{3}t^{3}]^{3}_{1}\\
		=&\frac{1}{2}([\frac{27}{3}]-[\frac{1}{3}])\\
		=&\frac{1}{2}(\frac{26}{3})\\
		=&\frac{13}{3}
	\end{align*}
	\subsection{RMS Value of a Function:}
	The RMS value of a function is the $2^{nd}$ root of the mean value over a given period.
	\\
	$$RMS=\sqrt{\frac{1}{b-a}\int^{b}_{a}[f(t)]^{2}dt}$$
	\\
	One of the main applications for this in alternating currents \& voltages between DC \& AC circuits.
	\subsection{Volume of Revolution:}
	Rotating a function (here $f(x)$) around an axis ($x$), producing a 3D shape. Most of the time makign a cone-like shape.
	\\
	$$V=\pi\int^{b}_{a}[f(x)]^{2}dx$$
	\\
	E.g. Find the volume of $y=2x$ for $0\leq x\leq 3$
	\begin{align*}
		V=&\pi\int^{3}_{0}[2x]^{2}dx\\
		=&\pi\int^{3}_{0}4x^{2}dx\\

	\subsection{Numerical Integration:}
	Numerical Integration is a set of principles to follow for equations that cannot be solved analytically (I.E. $\sin{(x^{2})}$). The two most common techniques to are approximation of the solution to the integration of the formula. These are the Trapezoidal and Simpson's Rule.
	\subsubsection{Trapezoidal Rule}
	$$\int^{b}_{a}f(x)dx\approx\frac{b-a}{2n}[f(x_{0})+2(f(x_{1})+f(x_{2})+\ldots+f(x_{n-1}))+f(x_{n})]$$
	\subsubsection{Simpson's Rule}
	Similar too the Trapezoidal Rules, Simpson's Rule provides an approximation that is considered even more accurate at width $h$ ($\frac{b-a}{n}$). It does this through utilizing the sum of multiqle quadratic equations.
	$$\int^{b}_{a}f(x)dx\approx\frac{h}{3}[f(x_{0})+4[f(x_{\text{odd}})]+2[f(x_{\text{even}})]+f(x_{n})]$$
	\subsection{Standard Ordinary Differential Equations:}
	\begin{align*}
		F_{1}=-ky\;\;&\;\;F_{2}=ma\\
		F_{1}=&F_{2}\\
		\therefore-ky=&ma\\
		a=&-\frac{ky}{m}\\
		\frac{d^{2}y}{dx^{2}}=&-\frac{ky}{m}\\
		m\frac{d^{2}y}{dx^{2}}+ky=&0
	\end{align*}
	Above is an example of a second-order differential equation.
	\subsection{Linear Differential Equations:}
	A differential equation is said to be linear, if and only if:
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item The dependent variable and derivative are of the first order (index 1)
		\item No products of dependent variable and derivative are present
		\item No non-linear functions are present within the equation
	\end{enumerate}
	\subsection{Seperation of Variables:}
	\begin{align*}
		\frac{dy}{dx}&=f(x)g(y)\\
		\int\frac{1}{g(y)}dy&=\int f(x)dx
	\end{align*}

	$$y(x)=\frac{\int\mu Q(x)dx}{\mu},\;\text{where}\;\mu=e^{\int P(x)dx}$$
